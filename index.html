My journey

From early college to leading the drivetrain.

I've always wanted to know how machines actually work: what happens between the engine firing and the wheels turning. Engineering is how I get to answer that with my own hands.

I graduated valedictorian of Navarro Early College High School through IBM's P-TECH program, which put me inside IBM as an intern straight out of high school. I've worked there as a co-op ever since, learning how real engineering organizations solve problems at scale.

At UT Austin I study Mechanical Engineering with a focus on Manufacturing and Design. In February 2026 I joined Longhorn Baja Racing as an ergonomics and powertrain member, designing the parts the driver touches: the steering wheel, hub, seat mount and brake bias bracket.

By October I was leading the gearbox project. By November that project had grown into a full 4WD system, and I was named Powertrain Co-Lead. Now I lead a 10-person team responsible for every part that moves power from the engine to the wheels.

Custom Yoke-Style Steering Wheel

The steering wheel is the one part the driver never lets go of. Mine is a compact yoke: two sculpted, angled grips joined by a braced center plate, so the driver gets a firm hold with gloves on and more room in a tight cockpit.

  • Designed in SolidWorks and refined through multiple revisions; this is REV02, V02.
  • Built as a system: wheel, handle grips, steering sleeve and a mating hub, with a manufacturing drawing.
  • Part of a cockpit redesign that cut driver interference 30% by re-angling the steering column and seat.

The 90° Box: turning power the corner to all four wheels

A four-wheel-drive Baja car needs to send power from the gearbox out to a driveshaft running the length of the car. The 90° box is the compact right-angle gearbox that does it, and I'm leading its design.

  • Turns the gearbox's output 90° into the driveshaft through a pair of steel bevel gears at a 1:1 ratio.
  • Module-4 gears chosen to match our gearbox, and sourced off the shelf so the team can build it on schedule with the tools we have.
  • Split casing (left and right halves) designed around one goal: the smallest possible package. The left half fits inside a 139 × 140 × 49 mm envelope.

Small-Engine Intake Redesign + Dynamometer

To prove an intake redesign worked, our 5-person team first had to build the instrument to measure it. We built a belt-and-drum brake dynamometer with a machined aluminum drum and an Arduino-based Hall effect speed sensor, then wrote interrupt-driven firmware that streamed data to MATLAB. The new intake used a 3D-printed manifold with a swept 45° bend and a lathe-turned 6061 aluminum velocity stack.
